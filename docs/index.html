What we built

Team 14 built this robot from the frame up at the UMass Robotics Club at UMass Amherst. It is an antweight combat robot, and almost every structural part is 3D-printed: the inner drive base that carries the motors and electronics, and the outer shell that doubles as the weapon. The frame went through several revisions across the build, each one printed, test-fit, and cut down to make room for hardware that turned out bigger than the model.

The weapon is the whole outer ring. Gear teeth are printed around its inner wall, and a pinion on the brushless motor meshes into them, so the entire shell spins around the drive base: a full-body shell spinner. Mass, armor, and weapon are all the same part, which is what makes the layout worth the packaging headache.

Everything electrical was hand-assembled. Motor leads, battery harness, connectors, and signal lines were soldered by the team and validated on a bench supply before anything was trusted to a LiPo. An ESP32 runs custom C++ firmware into two controllers: a dual-channel brushed board for the wheels, and a 35 A BLHeli_S ESC for the shell.

Control is an Xbox controller over Bluetooth. The bot was driven and weapon-tested on campus in spring 2026.

02

Build log

From bare motors on a bench in the fall to a driving machine the following spring, and the hardware as it stands today. Dated stages are anchored to the timestamps on the build photography.

FALL2025

Motors, power, and turning logic

The project started a full semester before the frame existed. Most of the fall went into fundamentals: driving bare motors from the ESP32, getting a battery wired in safely, and working out the turning logic so that a stick input produced the right response on each side. Getting two motors to spin the correct directions on command, repeatably, was the milestone everything afterward was built on.

APR 232026

Electronics bring-up

Before any of it went into a chassis, the ESP32 was brought up on a breadboard with the power harness built out separately: XT connectors, a switched battery lead, and motor lines fanned out so each could be probed on its own. The bench was set up with a soldering station and helping-hands for the connector work.

APR 262026

Power and the first rolling frame

Packs were balance-charged on a dedicated LiPo charger and checked before use, a habit that matters when the battery lives inside a machine that gets hit. The same day, the first version of the printed chassis was wired up and rolling on two wheels, with the electronics loose inside to check clearances.

MAY 012026

Drivetrain integration and bench validation

The full parts set was laid out and dry-fit: printed chassis with both brushed drive motors seated, ESP32, wheels, and the LiPo pack. Motors and switching were validated on a benchtop DC supply with current limiting rather than straight off a battery.

The weapon controller was the real problem. There was no proper board, and no guarantee one was ever coming, so the team pivoted and built their own: a switching circuit on a breadboard put together from a MOSFET, a diode and a resistor, driven by the ESP32 and proved against a motor scavenged on campus. Nobody on the team had built a motor controller before. It worked anyway. That circuit is what the clip below is running, and it carried the weapon channel until a proper kit, board and motor were finally secured. Nothing on the bot uses a MOSFET today.

MAY 082026

Firmware locked, machine driving

The working firmware was committed to Battlebots-Team-14 as the driving build: arcade drive with trigger throttle and brake, stick steering, ramped acceleration, a speed-capped shell on its own ramp, a latching emergency stop, and an automatic failsafe if the controller drops. The pulse-width constants in this build are the retuned set, replacing an earlier band that caused the drive to jam.

This is the machine that commit drove: green drive base carrying the wheel motors, ESP32, ESCs and pack, seated inside the toothed red shell. Fitting all of it inside the ring took cuts into the printed frame, the clearest thing the next revision needs to fix.

AUG 242026

Current condition

The bot as it stands today, between seasons. The underside is the clearest look at how the weapon works: gear teeth run the full inner circumference of the red shell, and the black pinion at the lower right meshes straight into them. The bolted green plate is the floor of the drive base, with a wheel visible at each end.

03

Control system

The firmware drives three PWM channels, two wheels and the shell, off the ESP32's LEDC hardware at 14-bit resolution and 50 Hz: a 20 ms period, the standard servo and ESC frame.

The bot is driven with an Xbox controller paired over Bluetooth, handled on the ESP32 by the Bluepad32 library. Because the input mapping is written against that library rather than against one specific pad, any Bluetooth controller Bluepad32 supports will drive the machine with no firmware change.

Platform and cost

The robot was costed as a modular kit rather than a one-off. Every build shares the same base: a brushed drive bundle, an ESP32, and a pack. From there a team chooses a weapon module, and the same firmware and drive base carry over either way.

Team 14 built the spinner configuration, which comes to $101.61 in parts. Buying the ESP32 boards twelve at a time brought the controller cost down to about $4 per robot.

05

What we learned

Every one of these came out of something that did not work the first time.

Build the part you cannot buy

There was no weapon ESC and no guarantee of getting one. With no prior experience building motor control circuits, the team designed and bench-proved their own from a MOSFET, a diode and a resistor, and ran the weapon on it. A missing part turned out to be a design problem, not a dead end.

ESCs arm on a pulse, not on silence

Writing a 0 duty cycle does not arm an ESC; it reads as no signal at all. The weapon ESC only came alive once it was fed a real 1.0 ms pulse and held there for two full seconds before anything else ran.

The first pulse band caused jamming

An earlier set of pulse-width constants put the deadband in the wrong place and the drive would bind. Retuning the band, and widening the neutral zone around 1228, fixed it.

Ramp the drive, not just the weapon

Stepping straight to full duty slams current and makes the bot undriveable. Ramping acceleration while letting deceleration stay fast gave a machine that is smooth to launch and still stops sharply.

Cap the weapon in firmware

The shell ceiling is a constant in code, not a mechanical limit. When the weapon is the entire outer ring, being able to lower a number and reflash was the difference between a testable machine and one nobody wanted to switch on.

Validate on a bench supply first

Current-limited bench power caught wiring mistakes that a LiPo would have answered with smoke. Nothing touched a pack until it had already run on the supply.

Never block the control loop

The loop runs on a 20 ms timer check rather than delay(), so controller input and the failsafe keep being serviced while motors update. A blocking delay in a combat robot means a bot that ignores the stop button.

Model the wiring, not just the parts

The frame was designed around the components and not the loom. Connector bodies, service loops and bend radius are what actually consumed the volume, and what forced cuts into finished prints.

Failsafe on disconnect is not optional

If the gamepad drops, the firmware neutralises drive and kills the weapon on its own. Without it, a Bluetooth dropout leaves a spinning shell that nobody is holding the controller for.

Where we pick it up

Current state, and the design guidance for the next revision.

The machine is complete and the firmware on main is the build that drove. Drive and weapon were both tested and working on campus in spring 2026, and nothing mechanical is known broken. Before the next run it needs a charged pack and a quick bench check.

  • Design v2

    Re-model the frame around the loomThe cuts we made to the printed frame were all to clear wiring and connectors, not parts. Model the harness, meaning connector bodies, service loops and bend radius, into CAD before printing, and design channels for it instead of trimming afterward.

  • Design v2

    Make it serviceable without unbalancing the shellGetting to the electronics currently means most of a teardown. Any access we add has to keep the ring balanced, because a spinning shell is unforgiving of asymmetric mass, so mirror any cutout on the opposite side or put the access in the drive base rather than the shell.

  • Design v2

    Treat the ring gear and pinion as wear partsThe entire weapon torque path runs through printed teeth. Inspect the ring and the pinion for rounded or chipped teeth before each run, keep the pinion shaft rigidly supported so mesh depth holds under load, and print spare pinions.

  • Design v2

    Strengthen where we cutEvery trimmed section is a thinner wall than the model intended. Reprint at the revised geometry rather than carrying forward parts that have been cut into, and reconsider infill and wall count on the shell specifically, since it is both the impact surface and the spinning mass.

  • Design v2

    Strain-relieve the solder jointsMuch of the assembly is hand-soldered and rides inside a machine built to take impacts. Anchor the leads so vibration and hits land on the chassis, not on the joints.

  • Firmware

    Re-tune the ramps if the pack changesThe accel, decel and shell-ramp constants were tuned against the original pack's sag. A cell with different internal resistance will feel different, so expect a short retune, and raise the shell cap only once drive behaviour is confirmed.

  • Nice to have

    Battery telemetryA voltage divider into a spare ADC pin and a low-voltage cutoff in the loop would let the bot look after its own pack mid-match, and flag a tired battery before it starts affecting drive.

  • Housekeeping

    Charge the packIt went flat sitting over the summer without a top-up. Balance charge it and check the cells before the next run.

  • Before driving

    Re-arm and re-verifyPower up on the bench with the bot propped so the wheels are off the ground and the shell is clear. Confirm both ESCs arm through the 2-second sequence, confirm the gamepad pairs, and test the O-button stop before the shell goes anywhere near full.
